The Double-Slit Mystery

Shoot things at a wall with two thin slits and see what lands on the screen behind it. Marbles and water waves act the way you'd expect. Electrons don't.

So what does it mean?

Tiny things follow different rules

An electron is not a tiny marble and not a water wave. It travels like a wave, spreading out and going through both slits. But whenever we detect it, it shows up in just one spot, like a particle.

Scientists call this wave–particle duality. Light, electrons, and even whole molecules behave this way. It's one of the main ideas of quantum mechanics, the physics behind lasers, computer chips, and MRI scanners.

“A phenomenon which is impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics.”Richard Feynman, physicist, about the double-slit experiment

Real experiments

  1. 1801Thomas Young shines light through two slits and sees stripes. This shows that light is a wave.
  2. 1905Albert Einstein explains that light also comes in small packets, which we now call photons.
  3. 1961Claus Jönsson does the double-slit experiment with electrons and finds stripes.
  4. 1989Akira Tonomura's team sends electrons one at a time. Just like in step 3, the dots slowly build up into stripes.

Words to know

Particle
A small object in one place, like a marble or a grain of sand.
Wave
A wiggle that spreads out as it travels, like ripples on a pond.
Interference
Two waves meeting: they add up where crest meets crest and cancel out where crest meets trough.
Electron
A tiny particle with a negative charge, found in every atom.
Measurement
Checking what something is doing. For tiny things, checking changes what they do.

For teachers: questions for discussion

  1. In step 3, can you tell where the next electron will land? What can you predict?
  2. Why don't we see stripes when we throw real marbles or baseballs?
  3. In step 4, the detector only looks. Why would looking change the pattern?

The patterns are simplified to make them clear. In real experiments, the spacing of the stripes depends on the wavelength and on how far apart the slits are. The detector in step 4 has to interact with each electron to find out which slit it went through, and that interaction is what destroys the stripes.
